## 4. Core BA Interview Q&A

### Role & fundamentals

**Q: What does a Business Analyst do?**

> A BA connects business problems to solutions. I elicit and clarify requirements, document processes and rules, define acceptance criteria, work with developers and testers, and make sure the final product — whether it's a system change, a dashboard, or a data pipeline — solves the actual business need. I also translate between technical and non-technical stakeholders so nothing gets lost in translation.

**Q: What's the difference between a BA and a Data Analyst?**

> A Data Analyst focuses on analysing data and producing insights. A BA focuses on the broader problem: why we need change, who is affected, what the process should be, and what the solution must do. There's overlap — I use data analysis to support requirements and validate outcomes — but the BA owns clarity of scope, stakeholder alignment, and delivery fit.

**Q: Walk me through your requirements gathering process.**

> 1. **Identify stakeholders** — who decides, who uses, who builds.  
> 2. **Clarify the business problem** — not "we need a dashboard" but "what decision is broken?"  
> 3. **Document AS-IS** — current process, pain points, data sources.  
> 4. **Define TO-BE** — outcomes, KPIs, success metrics.  
> 5. **Write requirements** — functional (what system/report must do), non-functional (performance, security), and data rules.  
> 6. **Validate** — walkthrough with stakeholders, sign-off.  
> 7. **Support delivery** — clarify during build, UAT, change requests.  
>
> Example: In my Monash public-health project I worked with research stakeholders to define data requirements and validation rules before building dashboards. In industry at Phoenix, tasks were mentor-assigned — smaller scope, but the same idea of clarifying what the output needed to show before building it.

**Q: How do you handle conflicting requirements from two stakeholders?**

> I bring them to the same conversation with the business problem written on the board. I restate each requirement in outcome language: "Sales wants daily revenue by region; Ops wants hourly fulfilment — what's the decision each supports?" Often the conflict is naming or granularity, not goals. If it's real conflict, I escalate with options and impact: "Option A serves Sales reporting; Option B serves Ops — here's cost and timeline for each." I document the decision and who approved it.

**Q: What goes in a BRD vs user stories?**

> **BRD (Business Requirements Document):** broader scope, business context, objectives, stakeholders, high-level functional/non-functional requirements, assumptions, constraints.  
> **User stories:** Agile format — "As a [role], I want [capability], so that [benefit]" with acceptance criteria.  
> I use BRD-style docs for larger clarity and user stories inside sprints for delivery. At Cognizant we worked in stories within agile cycles.

**Q: What are acceptance criteria? Give an example.**

> Clear, testable conditions for "done."  
> Example for a sales dashboard story:  
> - Dashboard shows daily revenue by region for last 90 days  
> - Data refreshes by 8am AEST  
> - Filters: region, product category, sales rep  
> - Totals reconcile to finance SQL report within 1%  
> - Accessible to Sales Managers role only  

**Q: How do you prioritise requirements (MoSCoW)?**

> **Must have** — launch fails without it.  
> **Should have** — important but can defer one sprint.  
> **Could have** — nice to have.  
> **Won't have (this release)** — explicitly out of scope.  
> I prioritise with the product owner/sponsor using business impact and dependency order.

**Q: How do you support UAT?**

> I prepare UAT scripts from acceptance criteria, identify business testers, set up test data, log defects with steps to reproduce, triage severity with dev/QA, and confirm fixes before sign-off. At Cognizant I was close to functional testing automation — same mindset: expected vs actual, evidence, retest.

**Q: How do you document a process (AS-IS / TO-BE)?**

> Interview process owners, observe if possible, draft flow (swimlane or simple steps), validate with them, note pain points and handoffs, then design TO-BE with removed steps, automation, or new data flows. For database work I used ER diagrams and data dictionaries — same principle: make the invisible visible.

## 5. Technical BA Q&A

### SQL (very likely — practice aloud)

**Q: Write a query to find the top 5 products by revenue last quarter.**

```sql
SELECT product_id,
       SUM(revenue) AS total_revenue
FROM sales
WHERE order_date >= DATE_TRUNC('quarter', CURRENT_DATE) - INTERVAL '3 months'
  AND order_date <  DATE_TRUNC('quarter', CURRENT_DATE)
GROUP BY product_id
ORDER BY total_revenue DESC
LIMIT 5;
```

*Note: Date syntax varies by database — say "I'd adjust date functions for Oracle/SQL Server/Postgres."*

**Q: What's the difference between INNER JOIN and LEFT JOIN?**

> INNER JOIN returns only matching rows from both tables. LEFT JOIN returns all rows from the left table plus matches from the right; unmatched right side is NULL. I use LEFT JOIN when I need all records from a master list even if there's no match in the other table — e.g. all customers even if they didn't order this month.

**Q: How would you investigate why a dashboard total doesn't match finance?**

> 1. Confirm definition — same date range, same filters, gross vs net.  
> 2. Compare row counts and distinct keys.  
> 3. Check for duplicates in joins (fan-out).  
> 4. Check null handling and excluded statuses.  
> 5. Run reconciliation SQL side-by-side.  
> 6. Document the root cause and fix the metric definition or ETL rule.  
>
> I did this mindset at Phoenix when improving reporting accuracy by 45% through validation pipelines.

**Q: What is normalisation? Why 3NF?**

> Normalisation reduces redundancy and update anomalies by organising data into tables with clear relationships. 3NF means no transitive dependencies — non-key fields depend only on the key. I applied this in a Monash database project across clinical, billing, and operational domains so reports didn't double-count or contradict.

### Data & reporting

**Q: How do you define a KPI?**

> A KPI is a measurable value tied to a business objective, with a clear owner, formula, data source, refresh frequency, and target or threshold. Bad KPI: "customer happiness." Good KPI: "% of support tickets resolved within 24 hours, sourced from ServiceNow, reviewed weekly by Ops Manager."

**Q: How do you design a dashboard for executives vs analysts?**

> **Executives:** few metrics, trend direction, red/amber/green, minimal filters, answer "are we on track?"  
> **Analysts:** drill-down, filters, export, detail tables, answer "why did this move?"  
> At Phoenix I built department dashboards with different views for managers vs operational teams.

**Q: What's the difference between a metric and a dimension?**

> **Metric (measure):** numeric — revenue, count, rate.  
> **Dimension:** category you slice by — region, product, time, department.

### Agile & tools

**Q: Describe your experience with Agile.**

I joined planning and refinement, clarified requirements during the sprint, helped triage production issues, and updated documentation in Confluence. I'm comfortable with Jira for tracking work and keeping requirements traceable to delivery.

**Q: What project management tools have you used?**

**30-second overview:**  
"I led a team delivering fraud monitoring on five million-plus e-commerce transactions — from requirements through to evaluation. My BA angle was defining what 'fraud' meant operationally, aligning on metrics, and reporting trade-offs between catching fraud and false alarms."

| Topic | Your answer |
|-------|-------------|
| **Business problem** | E-commerce platform loses money on fraudulent transactions; needs timely detection without blocking good customers. |
| **Your BA role** | Requirements gathering, feature definition (behavioural signals), success metrics, stakeholder reports on precision/recall trade-offs. |
| **Tools** | PySpark, Spark SQL, Kafka, Structured Streaming — Python ecosystem. |
| **Key metrics** | Precision, recall, confusion matrix, threshold tuning — false positives vs missed fraud. |
| **Team coordination** | Milestones, documentation standards, code review practices. |

**Follow-ups:**

- *"Explain precision vs recall for fraud."* — Precision: of flagged transactions, how many are actually fraud. Recall: of all fraud, how much we catch. High recall can mean many false positives — bad customer experience. BA helps business pick the threshold.  
- *"Batch vs streaming?"* — Batch: process historical chunks on schedule. Streaming: process events as they arrive for low-latency alerts. We used both — batch for training/analysis, streaming for live monitoring.

**If interviewer isn't technical:** Stay on business — "We flagged suspicious behaviour in seconds while measuring how often we wrongly blocked legitimate customers."
